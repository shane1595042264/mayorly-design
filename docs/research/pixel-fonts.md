### 2.1 I measured Stardew's actual English font

Using the community-unpacked `SmallFont.png` + `SmallFont.json` from `https://github.com/Tinuvile/Stardew_Valley_Cocos2d-x4.0` (`Resources/Stardew Valley Unpacked Resources/Fonts/`), cross-checked against the byte sizes in the local install:

- `LineSpacing: 33`, `Spacing: -1.0`, `DefaultCharacter: *`, 266 glyphs
- Alpha histogram of the atlas: only four values, `0`, `85`, `170`, `255`. **The atlas is anti-aliased**, not 1-bit.
- Every glyph bounding box has even dimensions (`H` 14x18, `x` 10x12, `g` 10x18, `M` 18x20, `i` 2x16), meaning the atlas is rasterized at **2x** the design size.

Dividing by 2 gives Stardew's actual Latin design metrics:

| Metric | Stardew SmallFont | Pixel Operator @16px |
|---|---|---|
| Cap height | 9 px | 9 px |
| x-height | 6 px | 7 px |
| Descender | 3 px | 2 px |
| Line height | 16.5 px | 16 px |
| Advance, `i` / `o` / `M` | 3 / 7 / 11 px | 5 / 7 / 9 px |
| Proportional | yes | yes |
| Stem weight | 1 px | 1 px |

I rendered a sentence using Stardew's own atlas and glyph table and put it directly above the same sentence in the candidate fonts. Stardew's face is a **light, 1px-stem, rounded, proportional pixel sans with a single-story `a` and open `g` tail**. Of everything tested, **Pixel Operator at 16px is by far the closest freely-licensed equivalent** in weight, proportion, and rhythm, slightly wider and slightly straighter in the terminals. m5x7 is lighter and more condensed than Stardew; m6x11 is much heavier; Departure Mono is monospace and therefore reads wider.

The practical upshot: chasing an exact Stardew match is a dead end (it is a rasterized 2x anti-aliased atlas of an unidentified face), but **Pixel Operator lands in the same visual family and on almost the same metric grid**, which is the achievable goal.

## 3. Licensing verdicts

- **1.3**: "Only the portions based on the Font Software are required to be released under the OFL. The intent of the license is to allow aggregation or bundling with software under restricted licensing as well." → your Rust/JS source stays closed.
- **1.4**: "Can I sell a software package that includes these fonts? Yes... games and entertainment software, mobile device applications, etc."
- **1.20**: "If you bundle a font under the OFL with your mobile app you must comply with the terms of the license. At a minimum you must include the copyright statement, the license notice and the license text." → put it in an About/Licenses screen.
- **1.6**: you may not sell the font *by itself*. Not a concern here.

### The three findings that decide the recommendation

**(a) Silkscreen has no lowercase whatsoever.** Not "small caps", not "lowercase that looks like caps". I compared the raw `glyf` coordinate arrays: for `a/A`, `g/G`, `p/P`, `q/Q`, `y/Y` the lowercase glyph outline is **byte-identical** to the uppercase, with identical advance widths (750, 750, 750, 750, 875). Rendering `"Morning! I sorted your dump into 2 chests."` in Silkscreen literally outputs `MORNING! I SORTED YOUR DUMP INTO 2 CHESTS.` This is fine for a `CHORES` header. It is disqualifying for a task list, where "email dr about knee" would become "EMAIL DR ABOUT KNEE" and lose all word-shape cues.

**(b) Press Start 2P has no descenders and no proportional spacing.** From the `glyf` table, `g`, `p`, `q`, `y` all have `yMin = 0`, i.e. they sit entirely on the baseline. Every single glyph has advance width exactly 1000/1000 em. At 8px that is 8px per character, so a 42-character sentence needs 336px; at 16px it needs 672px. Rendered side by side, a line that fits in Pixel Operator's 290px needs 672px in Press Start 2P. For an app whose whole job is dense text lists, this is a 2.3x line-length penalty *plus* mangled descenders.

**(c) Pixelify Sans has confusable digits.** I rendered `0123456789 Il1| rn m O0 S5 B8 2Z page qujy` in every candidate. In Pixelify Sans the `2` and `Z`, the `5` and `S`, and the `8` and `B` are near-indistinguishable at 16px, and `3` reads as an `S`. For an app that renders `Thu 9:00 AM`, `04 Sep 2026`, `7/12 done`, that is a real defect, not a taste objection. Pixelify Sans also has `grid = 1`, meaning its outlines are **not on a pixel grid at all** (it has softened/rounded pixel corners), so it will never render crisply at any size, it just looks pixel-ish.

By contrast, Pixel Operator at 32px (2x its native grid) shows: **slashed zero** distinct from `O`, `I` with top/bottom serifs distinct from bare `l` distinct from flagged-and-footed `1` distinct from full-height `|`, and clean `5/S`, `8/B`, `2/Z` separation, with real descenders on `p g q j y`.

**Jersey 10/15/25 fail the same test differently:** capital `I` and lowercase `l` render as identical plain vertical bars, and `0` and `O` are near identical. Fine for a sports-jersey display face, wrong for `Il1` in a to-do item.

Three consequences for todoFarm specifically:

1. **No pixel font in this entire survey has `✓` (U+2713) or `☐` (U+2610).** Zero of them. Your checkboxes and check marks **must** be sprites, CSS-drawn boxes, or glyphs from your own tilesheet. Do not design a UI that assumes a font checkbox exists.

## 6. The "one pixel font for everything" tradeoff, addressed directly

You asked me to address this with evidence rather than assert it. Here is the evidence.

**Text density at grid-aligned sizes** (characters per 100px of line width, from measured average lowercase advance):

| Font @ size | chars / 100px | cap height |
|---|---|---|
| m5x7 @16 | 17.7 | 7px |
| monogram @16 | 16.7 | 7px |
| m6x11 @16 | 14.7 | 11px |
| **Pixel Operator @16** | **14.4** | **9px** |
| Departure Mono @11 | 14.3 | 8px |
| VT323 @18 | 13.9 | 10px |
| Jersey 10 @18.667 | 13.5 | 10px |
| Tiny5 @16 | 13.1 | 10px |
| Pixelify Sans @16 | 10.9 | 10px |
| Silkscreen @16 | 8.4 | 10px |
| Press Start 2P @8 | 12.5 | 8px |
| Press Start 2P @16 | **6.25** | 16px |

**Where a single font genuinely fails.** The failure is not "pixel fonts are illegible", it is that the *display-oriented* pixel fonts (Press Start 2P, Silkscreen, Micro 5, Jersey, Bytesized, Coral Pixels) each drop one of the four features that long text requires: real lowercase, real descenders, proportional widths, or unambiguous digits. Once you require all four, the candidate list collapses to roughly Pixel Operator, Departure Mono, monogram, m5x7, and Tiny5. Of those, only Pixel Operator is proportional, CC0, punctuation-complete, and shipped with a metrically identical Bold.

**Why one font actually is the right answer here, with a caveat.** Pixel Operator ships Regular, Bold, Mono, Small Caps, Half-Bold, and an 8px variant, all from one designer on one grid (verified: `PixelOperator.ttf` and `PixelOperator-Bold.ttf` both measure em 16px / cap 9px / x-height 7px / descender 2px). That gives you a **type system, not a single font**: Bold for headers and speaker names, Regular for body, Small Caps for chest labels, all sharing one baseline grid so nothing shifts when you switch. That is exactly what usually forces people to mix two unrelated pixel fonts, and it is why the single-font answer works in this specific case where it usually would not.

**The caveat, stated honestly.** At 16px, Pixel Operator's 7px x-height is small for a macOS window where a user may read 30 task rows. Two mitigations, both verified against the metrics:
- Ship a **display-scale toggle** that switches the whole overlay between 16px and 32px (both are grid-aligned multiples of Pixel Operator's 16px em, so both are crisp). This is the same lever Stardew itself gives you with its zoom slider.
- For genuinely long-form text (an NPC lore dump, a settings explanation, an error message, a legal screen), it is legitimate and not aesthetically fatal to **drop out of the pixel font entirely** and use the macOS system stack. Reserve the pixel font for the diegetic surfaces (chests, dialogue box, task rows, HUD) and let non-diegetic chrome be normal text. Stardew does the analogous thing by rendering its Chinese build in `Noto Sans CJK SC Bold`, which is not a pixel font at all.

**If you want dialogue to feel different from lists.** Do not reach for a heavier pixel font (m6x11 is unlicensed and 124 glyphs; Tiny5 has an awkward `y` and `8/B` ambiguity). Instead:
- Same font, different **spacing**: dialogue at `line-height: 24px` with `letter-spacing: 1px`, lists at `line-height: 16px`, `letter-spacing: 0`. Departure Mono's own README recommends exactly this lever: "Experiment with tighter or wider tracking (letter-spacing)."
- Or use **Departure Mono @11px** for dialogue as a deliberate second voice. Its 8px cap / 6px x-height is a near-exact match for Stardew's own 9/6, and monospace reads as "someone is talking to you in a terminal", which suits a chatty NPC. Cost: 84 KB and wider lines.

## 7. Shipping notes for the Tauri v2 + Phaser stack

**Self-host, always.** Tauri v2 uses **WKWebView on macOS** (`https://v2.tauri.app/concept/process-model/`, footnote: "Currently, Tauri uses Microsoft Edge WebView2 on Windows, WKWebView on macOS and webkitgtk on Linux"). The Google Fonts CSS API is a network dependency and an offline-first local app must not have one. All the fonts above are redistributable; the `google/fonts` README explicitly says "Since all the fonts available here are licensed with permission to redistribute, subject to the license terms, you can self-host". Bundle the TTF in your Tauri resources and `@font-face` it from a local path.

**Crispness comes from grid alignment, not from CSS hacks.** Because these are true pixel-grid fonts, at integer multiples of the em/px value in §4 every outline edge lands exactly on a device pixel boundary and there is nothing for the rasterizer to anti-alias. Use `font-size: 16px` or `32px` for Pixel Operator, `11px` or `22px` for Departure Mono, `16px` or `32px` for m5x7/monogram, `8px` or `16px` for Silkscreen/Press Start 2P/Tiny5. **Do not use `rem`, `em`, or fractional sizes for pixel text**, and be careful with any CSS `transform: scale()` on a text container, which reintroduces subpixel positions.

Two traps worth knowing:
- **Jersey 10 cannot be rendered at whole pixels at an integer CSS size.** Its em is 1400 units with a 75-unit grid, so em/px is 18.667. Integer pixel modules only occur at `font-size: 56px` (3px modules) and multiples. This is a real, measured property of the font, not a rendering quirk.
- **Pixelify Sans, VT323, DotGothic16, Geist Pixel and Workbench have `grid = 1`**, meaning their outlines are not on any coarse pixel grid. They will *always* be anti-aliased. If a hard-edged pixel look is the goal, these are the wrong tool regardless of how they look in a specimen.

`font-smooth` / `-webkit-font-smoothing` is a fallback, not the plan. MDN flags it explicitly: "Non-standard: This feature is not standardized. We do not recommend using non-standard features in production" (`https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-smooth`). Reach for it only if grid alignment alone is not enough in your actual WKWebView build.

**Phaser side: use BitmapText, not Text.** Phaser's `Text` game object goes through canvas `fillText` and gets the browser's anti-aliasing. `Phaser.GameObjects.BitmapText` (`https://docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/class/gameobjects-bitmaptext`, current at Phaser v4.1.0) renders from a texture atlas and stays pixel-exact. Generate the `.fnt` + PNG atlas **from the exact same TTF at the exact same px size** you use in the DOM overlay, so a chest label in the world and a task row in the HTML panel are visually identical. This is precisely the architecture Stardew itself uses for its localized builds (`Russian.fnt` + `Russian_0.xnb` page texture), so you are following a proven pattern.

**Licenses screen.** OFL FAQ 1.20 requires the copyright statement, the license notice, and the license text for any bundled OFL font. CC0 requires nothing, but including the Pixel Operator attribution anyway is good practice and costs nothing. Put both in an About panel.

# Adversarial fact-check

## Corrections

**1. Pixel Operator's default line box is 16.72px at `font-size:16px`, not 16px.** §2.1's metrics table asserts "Line height | Pixel Operator @16px | 16px." Measured from the binary: `hhea` ascent 1300 / descent −300 / lineGap 72 on upem 1600 → 1672/1600 = **1.045em**, and `OS/2` sets USE_TYPO_METRICS with identical typo values. So `line-height: normal` yields 16.72px, a fractional line box that puts every baseline after the first off the pixel grid. §7 lists font-size, `rem`/`em`, and `transform: scale()` as the crispness rules and never mentions `line-height` — which is the other half of the rule. (Source: `pozip/PixelOperator.ttf` from `https://dl.dafont.com/dl/?f=pixel_operator`.)

**2. The "one grid, nothing shifts when you switch" family claim is false for half the family.** §6 says Pixel Operator's "Regular, Bold, Mono, Small Caps, Half-Bold, and an 8px variant" are "all from one designer on one grid… all sharing one baseline grid." Measuring all 15 faces in the official zip:

| Face | em/px | cap | x-ht | g descender | `line-height:normal` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PixelOperator / Bold / Mono / Mono-Bold | 16 | 9 | 7 | −2 | 1.045em |
| PixelOperatorSC / SC-Bold | 16 | 9 | 7 | **0** | 1.045em |
| PixelOperatorHB / MonoHB | 16 | 9 | 7 | −2 | **1.107em** |
| PixelOperator8 (+Bold/HB/Mono variants) | **8** | **7** | **5** | −1 | **1.090em** |

The Small Caps faces the report recommends for chest labels have **no descenders**; the Half-Bold faces have a different default line box; and the 8px cut is a different design proportion (cap 7 of an 8px em vs 9 of 16), not a scaled Pixel Operator. Only the four 16px non-HB faces genuinely interchange.

**3. "Metrically identical Bold" (§1) is true vertically, false horizontally.** Average lowercase advance is 6.92px (Regular) vs 7.85px (Bold) at 16px — a 13% difference, which the report's own §4 table records as 6.9/7.8. Baselines don't drift; line lengths do. The stated benefit ("no baseline grid drift between panels") survives; the wording overstates it.

**4. Phaser version is stale.** §7 says "current at Phaser v4.1.0." npm dist-tag `latest` is **4.2.1**, published 2026-07-09 (`https://registry.npmjs.org/phaser`). The docs site header does say 4.1.0, so the docs lag the package — but the report presents 4.1.0 as the current release.

**5. The Departure Mono glyph-coverage quote is attributed to the wrong file.** The report cites `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rektdeckard/departure-mono/main/README.md` for "1,186 glyphs, including support for: Basic Latin… Cyrillic, Basic Greek, Small caps…". That repo README is 66 lines / 2,244 bytes and contains none of that text (its only relevant lines are the tracking tip and a Licenses table). The quoted block is in the README **inside the v1.500 release zip**. Related trap the report should have flagged: the repo's root `LICENSE` is **MIT and covers the website**, not the font — the font's OFL sits at `public/assets/LICENSE`. §8 sends readers to the repo, where MIT is what they'll find first. The license *verdict* is correct: I extracted the zip and its `LICENSE` line 1 is verbatim `Copyright 2022–2024 Helena Zhang (helenazhang.com).` followed by SIL OFL 1.1, with no Reserved Font Name designation.

**6. Workbench "NO, 0/26 distinct" lowercase is a script artifact, not a fact.** 224 of Workbench's 237 glyphs are composites (`numberOfContours = -1`). The report's `outline_sig()` returns `('empty',)` for any glyph with `numberOfContours <= 0`, so all 26 lower/upper pairs compare equal by construction. Workbench has real lowercase: `a` yMax 626 vs `A` yMax 882, and rendering `agpy AGPY` shows distinct forms with descenders. (Silkscreen's 26/26 is genuine — it survives a stricter comparison including flags and advance widths.)

**7. Silkscreen is not on the 125-unit / 8-em-px grid the §4 table gives it.** 172 of 2,884 outline points (**6.0%**) are off multiples of 125 — the rounded glyphs (`0 6 8 9 @ A B D O P Q R a b d`…) carry 5-unit insets at coordinates like 130, 495, 245, 380, 620. The report defines "grid" as "the largest coordinate divisor that 99.9% of outline points are multiples of"; at 125 only 94% qualify, and the plain gcd that `analyze.py` actually computes returns **5** (em/px 200). The practical error is tiny (5/1000 em = 0.08px at 16px), but "Silkscreen at 8px or 16px is crisp by grid alignment" is not strictly true, and the stated method doesn't match the code.

**8. Coral Pixels deserves the Jersey 10 warning and doesn't get it.** §7 singles out Jersey 10 as unable to hit whole pixels at an integer CSS size. Coral Pixels is worse: upem 1000 / grid 77 → the smallest integer font-size giving whole-pixel modules is **1000px** (Jersey 10's is 56px). The §4 table lists it as a clean "em 13," which is a rounding of 12.987.

**9. Google Fonts pixel-tag count is 46, not 45.** `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/tags/all/families.csv` has 46 rows matching `/Theme/Pixel`. The report's own enumerated list also totals 46 — only the headline number is wrong.

**10. §4(c) "Jersey 10's `0` and `O` are near identical" is overstated.** Different widths (450 vs 525 units), different advances (525 vs 600), different corner treatment; rendered at 19px, `0` is a plain 6px bowl and `O` a 7px notched bowl. The `I`/`l` half of the claim is exactly right — byte-identical outlines, identical 225 advance.

**11. Minor:** Bytesized's descender is −1 module, not −2 (upem 16, grid 2, `g` yMin = −2 units). And §4's Pixel Operator `Il1|` description is inverted — rendered at 16px, **`I` is the bare bar and `l` carries the bottom-right foot**; `1` is flagged but not footed. They are all distinguishable, so the conclusion holds; the description doesn't.

## Omissions

1. **`font-synthesis: none`.** §7 is a crispness section that never mentions WebKit's synthetic faces. Any `font-weight: bold`, `<strong>`, or `<b>` without PixelOperator-Bold actually loaded makes WKWebView smear a fake bold; italic produces a sheared oblique. Both annihilate the pixel grid, and it's a one-line fix that belongs beside the font-size rule.

2. **devicePixelRatio / Retina.** Not mentioned once, in a macOS-first app. The entire grid-alignment argument assumes CSS px map to integer device px. True at DPR 2 (macOS default), not on macOS "scaled" display modes, fractionally-scaled external monitors, or a zoomed Tauri webview. §9 #5 says "verify in a Tauri build" without naming what to look for.

3. **Fallback stack and emoji.** The product's core input is arbitrary pasted user text, and the NPC *invents* chest names. Pixel Operator covers 238 codepoints. Emoji (near-certain in a todo app), CJK, Cyrillic, Greek, `✓`, `→`, `™` will either tofu or fall through to the macOS system font mid-line at wildly different metrics. The report diagnoses exactly this for m5x7's apostrophe, then never prescribes a `font-family` chain, a sanitization pass, or an emoji strategy for the font it actually recommends.

4. **Pan-CJK OFL pixel fonts exist.** §2 concludes multi-language pixel type is a dead end, citing Stardew's commercial Korean face. **Ark Pixel Font** (`https://github.com/TakWolf/ark-pixel-font`) ships pan-CJK + Latin at 10px / 12px / **16px** in both proportional and monospaced cuts, fonts under SIL OFL 1.1 with MIT build tooling (`LICENSE-OFL` returns 200). **Fusion Pixel Font** (`https://github.com/TakWolf/fusion-pixel-font`, OFL 1.1) is the same author's wider-coverage family. A 16px OFL pan-CJK grid lines up with Pixel Operator's own 16px em. This would change the report's i18n conclusion. (§9 #7 concedes search was unavailable, so this is a scoping gap rather than a false claim.)

5. **Trade dress, not just font licensing.** The stated constraint is "distributed, possibly commercially; licensing must be safe." §2 optimizes hard for getting as close as possible to Stardew Valley's typography and §3 clears the font licenses well, but nothing addresses that deliberately replicating a specific commercial game's UI look carries exposure independent of any font license.

6. **An unlicensed font sits in the headline recommendation table.** m6x11plus appears in §1's "Bottom line" splash-title row while §3 says do not build a commercial product on it without emailing the author. For a shipping binary the right move is to strike it from the recommendation table, not caveat it in place.

7. **CC0 is not literally "zero obligations."** CC0 expressly does not waive trademark or patent rights, and its waiver is not fully effective in every jurisdiction (it falls back to a permissive license). Practically fine here, but a shade strong for a document being used to clear a commercial ship.

8. **Accessibility.** A 7px x-height at 16px is a low-vision problem, and §7's "do not use `rem`, `em`, or fractional sizes" means the UI cannot respond to browser or system text scaling at all. The 16/32 toggle partly covers it but is never framed as the accessibility mitigation it is.

The measurement work is equally solid. Every file size is byte-exact, every cmap count reproduces (238 / 124 / 1079 / 226 / 1154 / 544 / 319 / 656 / 574 / 568 / 8231), every §6 density figure reproduces to the decimal (17.7 / 16.7 / 14.7 / 13.1 / 8.4 / 13.5 / 6.25), Silkscreen's 26/26 identical outlines with advances 750/750/750/750/875 is real, Press Start 2P's `yMin = 0` and single 1000 advance are real, Jersey 10's 1400/75/56px is exact, and no font in the survey has U+2713 or U+2610 (I also checked U+2611, U+2714, U+1F5F8 — absent everywhere). Roughly **90% trustworthy**. Act on the font choice and the licensing conclusions as written — they are well evidenced and I could not break them. Re-derive §7's shipping rules before implementing.
