# Asset pipeline: build or buy?

## Verdict

**No.** Nothing that exists does all seven steps, and the gap is not a feature here or there, it is structural and it sits exactly on the step you named most important. Across seven independently researched categories, roughly forty products, not one ships automatic validation of an uploaded image against an admin-declared per-slot spec. Every "validation" claim I could trace collapses into one of four things: a filetype allowlist and a max byte size (Cloudinary, Canto, PocketBase, Supabase, Payload), a metadata read-out shown to a human (PageProof Smart Check), an LLM advisory that explicitly never blocks (Ziflow ReviewAI, Filestage Review Agents, Artstash QC Agent), or a naming-convention regex on a text field misleadingly named "Input Validation" (Autodesk Flow Production Tracking). The second structural failure compounds it: almost no product in the market has a first-class object for art that does not exist yet. DAMs, proofing tools and pipeline managers all model assets that have been made; a slot is an empty placeholder carrying a spec, and without it there is no open queue to claim from (step 2) and no "unfilled" column on a dashboard (step 7). The only two categories that model unfilled work correctly are issue trackers and localization platforms, and neither of them can look at a pixel. So the honest shape of the market is: the workflow half exists in Crowdin and GitHub, the validation half exists nowhere as a product, and the validation half is the cheap half to write.

## The closest three

### 1. GitHub, public art repo, plus a validating Action

**Covers:** steps 1, 2, 4 and 7 essentially free. Issue forms carry the slot spec and now have a real drag-and-drop `upload` input ([form schema](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/using-templates-to-encourage-useful-issues-and-pull-requests/syntax-for-githubs-form-schema)). PR review is the admin approve/reject with competing branches as alternates. Projects is the catalogue dashboard. Uniquely, it is the only evaluated route where step 3 is **structurally pre-acceptance rather than advisory**: [protected branches](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-protected-branches/about-protected-branches) state that "Required status checks must have a `successful`, `skipped`, or `neutral` status before collaborators can make changes to a protected branch," so a failing Pillow script genuinely blocks the merge. Note that admins are exempt by default unless you opt them in.

**The exact disqualifier:** [ToS section D.5](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service) grants every user a worldwide licence to "use, display, perform and reproduce (by forking) Your Content." Going public makes your entire hand-drawn sprite catalogue, the actual differentiator of the game, permanently forkable, including by competitors. A restrictive LICENSE constrains downstream use, it does not switch off forking. Going private inverts everything: outside collaborators consume paid seats, anonymous asset fetch dies, and the fork-PR model that makes the whole thing work stops working for outsiders.

**Real price:** $0 at 20 contributors on a public repo, Actions free for public repos ([Actions billing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/managing-billing-for-your-products/managing-billing-for-github-actions/about-billing-for-github-actions)). Private is $4/user/mo Team, so **$80/mo at 20 artists**, or $420/mo on Enterprise ([pricing](https://github.com/pricing)).

**Three taxes to budget for regardless:** fork PRs run "with a read-only token and no access to secrets" ([GitHub engineering blog](https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/github-actions-improvements-for-fork-and-pull-request-workflows/)), so the bot **cannot post the rejection reason** without a `pull_request_target` footgun or a two-stage `workflow_run` split; "By default, all first-time contributors require approval to run workflows," so every new artist's first submission sits unvalidated pending a human click, at the moment of maximum drop-off; and assignment sits at Triage level in [repository roles](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization), so **self-claiming is impossible** without a `/claim` bot you write.

### 2. Payload CMS, self-hosted

**Covers:** the cleanest verified pre-persist gate of any backend evaluated. In current source, `packages/payload/src/collections/operations/create.ts` runs file metadata generation **before** the `beforeValidate` hooks, and `getBaseFields.ts` puts `width` and `height` on every upload collection, so `data.width`/`data.height` are populated and `req.file.data` holds the raw buffer when your hook fires. A blocking per-slot validator with a specific rejection reason is definitely buildable. MIT licence, no seats, so 20 artists cost $0 ([LICENSE.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/payloadcms/payload/main/LICENSE.md)). Access control gives you filtered public read for step 5 in config, not code ([access control docs](https://payloadcms.com/docs/access-control/overview)).

**The exact disqualifier:** only one auth collection can reach the admin panel ([admin overview](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/payloadcms/payload/main/docs/admin/overview.mdx)), so your 20 artists get **no UI at all**. The open-slot queue, the claim button, the upload form and the rejection display are a frontend you build from zero. Payload's own upload validation is `mimeTypes` and `fileSize`, both declared per collection, so **not one of your six spec fields can be expressed in its config**, not even dimensions.

**Real price:** $0 licence plus roughly $15 to $40/mo infrastructure. `payloadcms.com/pricing` returns **HTTP 404** and Cloud signups appear paused after the June 2025 Figma acquisition, so any circulating $35/$199/$833 figures are stale scrapes of a dead page. Do not budget on them.

**Pixel-art-specific trap:** Payload pipes uploads through sharp, which re-encodes. [Issue #6951](https://github.com/payloadcms/payload/issues/6951) stripped metadata from the original image, [#13499](https://github.com/payloadcms/payload/issues/13499) changed file sizes. Both are fixed, but do not configure `imageSizes` or `resizeOptions` on the art collection, pin the version, and add a round-trip byte test.

### 3. Directus, self-hosted

**Covers:** steps 4, 5, 6 and 7 better than anything else here. Auto-generated REST and GraphQL over every collection, a Public policy for unauthenticated reads, real approval workflows, and `directus_files` already stores `width`, `height`, `filesize`, `type` ([files API](https://directus.com/docs/api/files)).

**The exact disqualifier, and it is a silent one:** `files.upload` is an **action** event only, never a filter, and the docs state "The `files` collection does not emit a `create` or `update` event on file upload" ([hooks](https://directus.com/docs/guides/extensions/api-extensions/hooks)). A collaborator answered the blocking question directly in [discussion #8768](https://github.com/directus/directus/discussions/8768): "I am afraid you can't." The feature request for exactly your need, [#21568](https://github.com/directus/directus/discussions/21568), was closed June 2024 for insufficient community votes. Worse, [Directus 12 introduced active licence enforcement](https://directus.com/docs/releases/breaking-changes/version-12) and on an unlicensed Core instance "custom rules on access policies will be ignored." That is the exact mechanism you would use to stop an artist setting `status = approved` on their own submission. It does not error, it just stops applying.

**Real price:** $0 under the [Open Innovation Grant](https://directus.com/oig) if you are under $5M revenue and 50 employees, but it is a licence you apply for and renew annually. Otherwise Core is a hard 3 seats, and Team is $499/mo billed annually plus $50/seat/mo past 10, so roughly $12,588/yr at 21 seats ([pricing](https://directus.com/pricing)).

**Runners-up worth one line each:** Keystone 6 (6/10) validates dimensions and format in a `validate` hook for about ten lines, but `getImageMetadata` returns only `{width, height, extension}` from the first 1024 bytes, so colour count, alpha and frames are invisible, and `storage.put` cannot see which slot it is serving. Supabase (5.5/10) is the cheapest honest substrate at ~$300/yr, but Sharp is **documented as unsupported** in Edge Functions, so your validator runs WASM-only inside 256MB and a hard 2s CPU ceiling. PocketBase (5/10) has the cleanest true-blocking hook of any hosted-shape backend but ships an admin-only UI and is pre-1.0 with the project stating "There are no promises for maintenance and support."

**Two things to strike off the list entirely.** Pixel Art Lint is v0.1.4, its own docs page returns 114 bytes, its "palette" rule is a hex allowlist rather than a colour-count cap, it has no alpha rule and no frame rule (roadmapped to 0.4.0), it emits only human-readable text plus an exit code, the binary has **no stated software licence**, and the vendor's own contact page says "We are still pre-release: nothing here ships as a finished product yet." And free-tex-packer-core silently swallows read errors, so a corrupt PNG produces a **green build with a missing sprite and `error == null`**; it is usable as an atlas step only if you wrap it and diff frame keys.

## What to steal

The localization platforms got the workflow right, and the shape maps onto ours almost one to one. Copy these, verbatim where you can.

**Crowdin's string model is our submission model with the nouns swapped.** Many contributors submit competing candidates against one key, peers vote, and a proofreader with elevated permission approves exactly one, while the losers persist as visible, retrievable alternates rather than being deleted. That is precisely requirement 4. Steal the data shape: a slot is a key, a submission is a candidate translation, approval is a separate role acting on a separate step.

**Separate the submit step from the review step as first-class workflow stages with different roles.** Crowdin's translate-step versus proofread-step split, with different member roles assignable per step, is the right model for artist versus admin, and it is what makes "browse open slots" and "review what passed validation" two different screens with two different permission sets rather than one CRUD table with a status column.

**Steal the rejection-error contract, which is the single most transferable idea.** Crowdin's File Pre-Import Processing module returns `{"error": {"message": "..."}}` and the docs describe it as "visible to a user in the UI." A machine-readable rejection rendered in the contributor's own upload screen at upload time is exactly the UX step 3 requires. Their engine can only produce it for text; ours produces it for pixels. The API shape is the same.

**Steal Lokalise's async upload pattern.** Upload returns a background process id you poll for status. Correct for a validator that may take a second or two per frame, and it stops you from designing a synchronous endpoint you will have to tear out.

**Steal Transifex's "review once or twice" toggle** as the right granularity of knob for whether a given slot needs a second pair of eyes. Hero sprites get two reviewers, background tiles get one.

**From outside localization, three more.** Roblox's validation-system documentation is the best available specification of what good failure feedback looks like: name the failing check, say how to fix it, say why it matters, ship a visualiser. Minecraft Creator Tools' rule catalogue is a directly copyable output format, with rule IDs, severity of Error or Warning, an auto-fix column, and failure payloads carrying the offending path plus the actual observed dimensions. And Faithful, a volunteer Minecraft texture-pack project, is the only place in the entire survey where a slot registry with stable ids, community submission against a specific slot, two-stage review with instapass and invalidate, automatic publication into the shipping pack, and a public manifest API all coexist. Read its architecture. It is AGPL-3.0, so read it, do not vendor it.

**And one thing nobody has, so there is nothing to steal:** self-serve claiming with an expiry. Crowdin tasks are manager-push, Weblate uses component locking, GitHub cannot let a Read-role user self-assign. Claim-with-TTL is net new in every candidate. Design it yourself and expect to iterate on the timeout.

## If we build it

### The minimum viable version

Five tables and one endpoint. `slots` with the spec as JSONB. `claims` with an artist id and an expiry timestamp. `submissions` with a file reference, the validation report as JSONB, the licence grant, and a status. `users`. `manifest_revisions`. One upload endpoint that runs the validator server-side, writes to a quarantine prefix, and promotes to the public prefix only on pass. One admin page showing competing submissions side by side against the spec. Two public GETs: a manifest filtered to approved, and a slot-status list for the dashboard. One artist page: open slots, claim, drag-and-drop upload.

Cut from v1: atlas generation (ship individual PNGs plus a manifest, add packing as a later build step), animation frame tags, anchor rules beyond a stored integer pair, notifications, and any alternates gallery richer than a list.

### What looks hard and is not

**PNG validation.** All four checks are trivial, and they are trivial *in the browser*, which means the artist gets the specific reason before a single byte reaches your server. Draw the image to a canvas and call `getImageData`, and you get a flat `Uint8ClampedArray` of RGBA quads:

- **Dimensions:** `img.naturalWidth`, `img.naturalHeight`. One comparison against the slot spec.
- **Alpha:** scan every fourth byte for a value other than 255. Canvas normalises everything to RGBA, which sidesteps the trap that bites server-side decoders: an indexed PNG carrying transparency via a `tRNS` chunk reports mode `P` with no `'A'` in the mode string, so a naive Pillow check rejects legitimate Aseprite exports. The browser does not have this problem.
- **Palette count:** pack each pixel into a uint32 and drop it in a `Set`. Size of the set is the colour count. Two decisions you must make and then publish to artists, because the same file passes or fails depending on them: does a fully transparent pixel count as a colour, and does the same RGB at two different alpha values count as one colour or two. On a test image, four pixels gave `RGBA distinct: 4, RGB-only distinct: 2`. Write the rule into the slot spec as an explicit flag.
- **Frame count:** integer-divide sheet width by declared frame width, or parse the APNG `acTL` chunk for animated files.

That is an afternoon, plus fixtures. Three independent existence proofs confirm the rules are cheap: BUIValidator (CC0) enforces texture size, compression, pixel format, mip settings and naming prefixes in Unreal; Pixel Art Lint enforces palette, colour mode and size from a TOML and fails a CI build; and Lospec auto-scans every gallery submission against its palette database at upload time.

**The one caveat that matters:** browser validation is UX, not enforcement. Cloudinary's Upload Widget proves the failure mode, its `minImageWidth`/`minImageHeight` genuinely cancel an upload, and the docs are explicit that it is client-side only, so a `curl` POST walks straight past it. Run the identical logic server-side as the actual gate. Same rules, same JSON report format, two execution sites. Server-side, add a decompression-bomb guard (Pillow ships `MAX_IMAGE_PIXELS` and `DecompressionBombError`, you have to catch it) and pass `maxcolors` explicitly to `getcolors`, which returns `None` above 256 and will otherwise throw a `TypeError` on exactly the oversized-palette input it is meant to reject.

### What is actually hard

**Identity and moderation at scale.** This is the real cost and it never appears in a feature comparison. Anonymous strangers uploading binaries to your service means Sybil accounts, claim squatting, spam, and the need for a human moderation queue for the art that *passes* the machine gate but is traced, offensive, or AI-generated. GitHub's first-run workflow approval default exists precisely because executing on stranger-authored content is dangerous, and it is the single friction point most likely to lose you contributors. You will need email plus OAuth identity, per-artist rate limits, a ban list, claim TTLs with automatic release, and a policy for what happens when an artist claims fifteen slots and finishes none. No surveyed tool gives you any of this.

**Licence provenance.** Not one product in any of the seven categories models licence or attribution as a first-class field. Not the DAMs, not the pipeline tools, not the proofing platforms, not the localization platforms whose whole business is crowd contribution. OpenGameArt has licences but they are all open by design, which is wrong for a commercial closed-source ship. You need a versioned CLA accepted at claim time, timestamped and immutable per artist per version, a per-submission licence grant record, and an attribution string that survives into the shipped build. Note that the atlas format will not carry it: free-tex-packer's `rects` has a fixed schema with no passthrough, so custom fields attached to input sprites come back empty. Attribution lives in a sidecar joined post-pack. Get this wrong and it is the failure that surfaces two years after launch, in a dispute, not in a bug report.

**Storage and CDN.** Rejected uploads still cost inbound bytes and must never land in the public prefix, which is exactly why the whole post-hoc-delete tier of backends (Directus, Appwrite, Firebase, Supabase webhooks) is the wrong shape. Quarantine prefix, promote on pass, GC the rest. Then decide public bucket versus signed URLs, and watch the read side: GitHub's unauthenticated limit is **60 requests per hour per IP**, now covering `raw.githubusercontent.com` too, which is fine for one atlas per build and fatal for 400 individual sprite fetches from a shared CI IP.

**Atlas generation.** Cloudinary's sprite generation, the only automated packer in the DAM category, is deprecated as of 16 September 2025. free-tex-packer-core is MIT, still maintained (0.3.9, July 2026, 16k weekly downloads) and fine as a build step, but it **silently drops unreadable files and reports success**, so you must wrap it and hard-fail on a frame-key diff. Packing must also be deterministic so the atlas does not reshuffle on every build, and `padding`/`extrude` need pixel-art-safe values. Adopt the Aseprite `json-array` shape for the manifest, because Unity's first-party importer, Godot plugins, Phaser and PixiJS already read it.

**Versioning approved art without breaking builds.** The hardest problem and the one with the least prior art anywhere in the survey. A stable asset id must survive an artist redrawing an approved sprite. Game builds must pin a manifest revision, never fetch "latest," or an admin approving an alternate at 2am silently changes a build that already shipped. You want content-addressed asset URLs, an immutable manifest per release tag, and a changelog of which slots moved between tags. Cloudinary's public list-JSON is the only public prior art I found for a fetchable manifest and it has no revision concept at all.

Realistic effort for v1 on a stack you already know: three to six weeks for one engineer, then permanent ownership of a public upload service. That is my estimate, not a sourced figure.

## Recommendation

**Build it, but not yet. Start on GitHub, and set an explicit trigger for when to switch.**

The reason to build is simple arithmetic: five of the seven steps are absent from every product surveyed, the sixth (licence provenance) is absent from all forty, and the one step everyone lacks is the one that takes an afternoon in `getImageData`. There is no purchase that gets you to 70% and leaves you 30% of glue. Every option leaves you writing the majority, so pay for a substrate you understand rather than for a licence that also constrains you. If you build today, use Postgres plus whatever framework you already run in production, with S3-compatible storage, at roughly $300/yr. Payload is the best-verified head start if you want the schema, auth, admin CRUD and access control handed to you, at the cost of building the entire artist-facing frontend anyway.

**The cheapest credible alternative, and when it is genuinely the better answer.** A public GitHub repo, one directory per slot, an issue form per slot carrying the spec, a required status check running a fifty-line Pillow validator, Projects as the dashboard, and a small bot for `/claim` and for relaying rejection reasons. This costs $0, and it gives you steps 1, 2, 4 and 7 for free plus the only structurally pre-acceptance gate in the entire survey. It is the better answer when all three of these hold:

1. The founder is willing to publish the sprite catalogue publicly and accept [ToS D.5](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service) forking rights over it.
2. The catalogue is under roughly 100 slots and the contributor pool is small enough that a first-time-contributor approval click per new artist is tolerable.
3. Your artists will accept a web form plus a bot, or are already comfortable with a fork-and-PR flow.

Switch to building when any one of those breaks. Concretely: **the moment the answer to "can the art repo be public?" is no.** At that point GitHub costs $80/mo at 20 artists, anonymous asset fetch stops working so the game build needs a token, and the fork-PR model that made the whole thing free stops working for outside contributors, which is to say you are paying more for less than a service you own. The second trigger is the first non-technical artist who abandons a submission at the git step, and the third is the first time you need to answer "which sprite was in build 1.4.2" and cannot.

Do not wait for Pixel Art Lint. Do not buy a DAM. Do not buy a proofing tool. Write the validator first, in the browser and on the server, because it is the cheapest part of the system and it is the part that determines whether any of the rest is worth building.
